### What I learned
- Wi-Fi encryption uses keys available to authorized clients, not only the router; it still prevents a casual nearby observer from simply reading traffic.
- SNI helps a server choose the correct website and certificate when several sites share an IP address. It is not what routes traffic to the server—that is the destination IP’s job.
- QUIC was originally developed by Google, standardized by the IETF, and integrates TLS 1.3 over UDP.

### The Story: The Office Building

You (your laptop) are sitting in a giant office building (the Wi-Fi network). You want to send a letter to a client downtown (a web server).

**1. The Public Wi-Fi Walls (Why you can't see others' mail)**
You look around the office. You see other people typing. Why can't you just reach over and read their letters? 
*   **WPA2 Encryption:** Everyone is writing in a secret code that only the building manager (the router) knows. You can see the paper, but it looks like gibberish.
*   **Client Isolation:** The building manager has instructed everyone: "Do not pass letters directly to each other. You must only hand letters to me, and I will pass them out."
*   **Switched Networks:** Even if you tried to grab a letter, the building manager hands it directly into the recipient's locked mailbox. You physically cannot reach it.

**2. ARP Spoofing vs. Port Mirroring (How to intercept the mail)**
*   **ARP Spoofing (The Attacker):** You stand up and yell, "Hey everyone, I am the new building manager! Give all your letters to me!" Everyone hands you their mail. You read it, then run it to the real manager so the internet doesn't break. It works, but you are being very loud and obvious.
*   **Port Mirroring (The Admin):** The real building manager lets you install a tiny camera over the mail-sorting desk. You see every letter, silently, without disrupting anyone. (This is why admins use SPAN—it's silent and safe).

**3. DNS (The Phonebook)**
You want to send a letter to `phantom.app`. But you don't know the exact street address. So, you shout over the intercom to the building directory (DNS): *"Where is `phantom.app` located?"* The directory shouts back: *"It's at IP address 104.18.35.227!"*
*(You saw this happen in Wireshark over UDP Port 53. UDP is like a quick shout—no confirmation needed, it just fires the answer back).*

**4. The TCP Handshake (The Phone Call)**
Before you send the letter, you have to make sure the client downtown is actually listening. So you call them on the phone.
*   **You (SYN):** "Hello? It's me. Can we talk?"
*   **Server (SYN-ACK):** "I hear you. Yes, we can talk."
*   **You (ACK):** "Great, here comes the letter!"
*   **Why IP Spoofing fails:** If you prank-called them and pretended to be someone else (IP Spoofing), they would say "Yes, we can talk!" *to the person you are pretending to be*, not to you. You would never hear the confirmation, so the conversation would die.

**5. TLS and SNI (The Sealed Envelope)**
Now you are talking to the client. But the mailroom has a rule: every letter must be placed inside a heavy, locked safe (TLS Encryption). You and the client share the only key. 
*   **The Catch:** The mailroom needs to know *which building* to send this safe to. So, you write the name `phantom.app` on the *outside* of the safe in plain marker. 
*   **SNI:** The plain marker on the outside of the lockbox is the **SNI**. Anyone looking at the mailroom can see you are sending mail to `phantom.app`, but they can't see the letter inside the safe. 

**6. QUIC (The Speedboat)**
For years, all letters went by train (TCP), which required a strict schedule and checkpoints. But recently, Google invented a speedboat (QUIC) that travels on the water (UDP). It's much faster, but it still uses the same locked safes (TLS) to protect the letters.
